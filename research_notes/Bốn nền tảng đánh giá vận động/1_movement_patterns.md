# Movement Patterns (Mẫu chuyển động) in PT, Rehabilitation and S&C

Scope note: this file covers definitions/classification of fundamental movement patterns, standardized screening tools (FMS, SFMA, NASM OHSA, LESS, Y-Balance), their reliability/validity/injury-prediction evidence, common faults, and the APTA "movement system" identity. Symmetry/asymmetry, ROM norms and motor control are covered by other researchers and only mentioned in passing. Research done 2026-10-16; ~20 tool calls; several sources were paywalled (flagged in Gaps).

---

## 1. How are fundamental movement patterns defined and classified, and by whom?

### Takeaway
There is no single authoritative, consensus taxonomy of "fundamental movement patterns." Two parallel traditions exist: (a) the S&C/coaching taxonomy (squat, hinge, lunge, push, pull, carry, ± rotation/anti-rotation, gait), which comes mainly from practitioner literature (Dan John and others), not from an NSCA/ACSM position stand; and (b) the PT/clinical tradition, where "fundamental movements" are operationalized through screens (Cook's FMS: 7 patterns) and the APTA "movement system" concept, plus diagnostic classification models such as Sahrmann's Movement System Impairment (MSI).

### Cited Findings
**S&C / coaching taxonomy (practitioner sources, NOT peer-reviewed — flag as weak evidence)**
- Most coaching sources converge on a core set of squat, hinge, lunge, push, pull and carry, with rotation often added as an extra/"bonus" pattern — [Evan Arbour, "The 6 Fundamental Movement Patterns" (Substack)](https://evanarbour.substack.com/p/the-6-fundamental-movement-patterns); [Breaking Muscle](https://breakingmuscle.com/go-beyond-the-5-fundamental-movement-patterns/)
- Lists vary in size: Dan John's five are squat, hip hinge, push (press), pull (row) and loaded carry; John Rusin adds a sixth (the lunge); other coaches drop the lunge and add rotation and counter-rotation (anti-rotation); some lists have eight, adding "hang" — [Breaking Muscle](https://breakingmuscle.com/go-beyond-the-5-fundamental-movement-patterns/); [Dr John Rusin](https://drjohnrusin.com/?p=17776); [Barpath Fitness](https://barpathfitness.com/blog/eight-foundational-movement-patterns-to-include-in-your-training/)
- Working definitions used in this tradition: hinge = hip-dominant movement with a neutral spine (deadlift, kettlebell swing); squat = knee- and hip-dominant with deeper knee flexion and lower descent; lunge = the only pattern performed from an asymmetric (split) stance, hence its carry-over to single-leg loading — [Strength for Longevity (Substack)](https://strengthforlongevity.substack.com/p/exercise-series-the-hinge-and-squat); [Evan Arbour](https://evanarbour.substack.com/p/the-6-fundamental-movement-patterns)
- The search did not surface any NSCA- or ACSM-published classification of "fundamental movement patterns"; the taxonomy appears to be practitioner consensus — (search of NSCA-related terms returned only coaching blogs; see Gaps).

**Clinical / screening tradition (Cook)**
- The FMS operationalizes "fundamental movement patterns" as 7 tests: Deep Squat, Hurdle Step, In-Line Lunge (Part 1) and Shoulder Mobility, Active Straight Leg Raise, Trunk Stability Push-Up, Rotary Stability (Part 2) — [Cook G, Burton L, Hoogenboom BJ, Voight M. IJSPT 2014;9(3):396–409, PMC4060319](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/); [Cook et al. Part 2, IJSPT 2014;9(4):549–563, PMC4127517](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4127517)
- Cook et al. frame screening as checking whether a person moves above a "minimal standard" rather than "perfectly," and describe a proximal-to-distal developmental sequence (infants stabilize proximal joints before distal) and the need for a balance of mobility and stability — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)
- The original FMS papers state fundamental movement screening can be done without trademarked equipment — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)

**Movement system / diagnostic classification tradition (Sahrmann, APTA)** — see Q5 for detail.
- Sahrmann's MSI model originally proposed five low back pain categories (lumbar flexion, extension, rotation, rotation-with-extension, rotation-with-flexion); factor analysis of 188 patients supported 3 of the 5 categories (rotation-with-extension, rotation, extension), not directly the flexion category — [Van Dillen et al., "Movement system impairment-based categories for low back pain: stage 1 validation" (WUSTL profile)](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/movement-system-impairment-based-categories-for-low-back-pain-sta/)

### Inferences
- For an app, a pragmatic pattern taxonomy would be: Squat, Hinge, Lunge/Split-stance, Push (horizontal/vertical), Pull (horizontal/vertical), Carry (loaded locomotion), Rotation / Anti-rotation (trunk), Gait/Locomotion, plus Landing/Jumping (needed for LESS). This is a synthesis of practitioner lists, not an officially endorsed standard — label it as such in the UI.
- Mapping the FMS 7 tests onto that taxonomy: Deep Squat → Squat; In-Line Lunge → Lunge; Hurdle Step → single-leg stance/gait component; Trunk Stability Push-Up → Push/anti-extension; Rotary Stability → Rotation/anti-rotation; ASLR and Shoulder Mobility → mobility components rather than patterns per se.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm an NSCA (Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning, 4th ed., Haff & Triplett 2016) or ACSM definition/classification of "fundamental movement patterns." The textbook's exercise-technique chapters are organized by exercise, and I could not verify a formal pattern taxonomy online. Treat "NSCA defines X patterns" claims as unverified.
- Could not retrieve Sahrmann's textbooks (Diagnosis and Treatment of Movement Impairment Syndromes, 2002; Movement System Impairment Syndromes of the Extremities, Cervical and Thoracic Spines, 2011) directly; category names for other regions (e.g., hip, shoulder syndromes) are not verified here.
- Gait as a "pattern" is well established clinically but not covered by the screening tools above; no source fetched.

## 2. Standardized assessment frameworks: items, scoring and cut-offs

### Takeaway
FMS is the most precisely specified (7 tests, 0–3 each, total 0–21, 3 pain clearing tests, lower side counts; ≤14 is the commonly used "risk" cut-off from early studies). SFMA is a clinical (pain-population) extension with 10 "top-tier" movements scored categorically. NASM OHSA is a non-numeric compensation checklist. LESS is a 17-item jump-landing error count (higher = worse; ≥5 was the ACL cut-point in one youth soccer cohort). YBT-LQ is a reach test with composite and asymmetry thresholds (asymmetry belongs to another researcher).

### Cited Findings
**Functional Movement Screen (FMS) — commercial (Functional Movement Systems, Cook/Burton); scoring published in peer-reviewed IJSPT**
- General rules: score 0–3 per test; bilateral tests are scored on both sides and the LOWER score is recorded; max total = 21; "score low when in doubt"; a score of 0 is given if pain occurs anywhere during the test, and the clinician is directed to the SFMA — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/); [Cook et al. 2014 Part 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4127517)
- Deep Squat: 3 = torso parallel to tibia or toward vertical, femur below horizontal, knees aligned over feet, dowel aligned over feet; 2 = completes only with heels elevated on a 2x6 board (or with compensation); 1 = cannot complete even with heels raised / lumbar flexion / tibia and torso not parallel — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)
- Hurdle Step (per side): 3 = hips, knees, ankles aligned in sagittal plane, minimal lumbar movement, dowel stays parallel to hurdle; 2 = loss of alignment, lumbar movement, or dowel not parallel; 1 = foot contacts hurdle or loss of balance — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)
- In-Line Lunge (per side): 3 = dowel stays in contact with spine and vertical, no torso movement, dowel and feet stay in sagittal plane, back knee touches board behind front heel; 2 = any of those lost; 1 = loss of balance — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)
- Shoulder Mobility (per side; hand length = distal wrist crease to tip of 3rd finger): 3 = fists within 1 hand length; 2 = within 1.5 hand lengths; 1 = > 1.5 hand lengths; if exactly on the boundary score low — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4127517)
- Active Straight Leg Raise (per side): 3 = malleolus between mid-thigh and ASIS; 2 = between mid-thigh and knee joint line; 1 = below knee joint line — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4127517)
- Trunk Stability Push-Up (body must rise as one unit, no lag in lumbar spine): 3 = men with thumbs at top of forehead / women at chin; 2 = men at chin / women at clavicle; 1 = unable at the level-2 position — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4127517)
- Rotary Stability (quadruped, per side): 3 = correct unilateral (same-side arm/leg) repetition; 2 = correct diagonal repetition; 1 = unable to perform diagonal repetition — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4127517)
- Clearing tests (pain yes/no only; positive → associated test = 0): shoulder impingement clearing test → Shoulder Mobility; spinal extension (press-up) clearing → Trunk Stability Push-Up; spinal flexion (quadruped rock-back) clearing → Rotary Stability — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4127517)
- ≤14 cut-off origin as cited by Cook et al.: Kiesel et al. reported athletes scoring ≤14 had dysfunctional patterns possibly linked to injury risk; Chorba et al. reported female collegiate athletes scoring ≤14 had ~4-fold higher lower-extremity injury risk (OR 3.85–4.58) — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)

**Selective Functional Movement Assessment (SFMA) — commercial (same company); for people WITH pain**
- SFMA has 10 "top-tier" movements scored with a categorical system (functional non-painful, functional painful, dysfunctional non-painful, dysfunctional painful — FN/FP/DN/DP); the FMS sends painful patterns to the SFMA — [Stanek, Smith & Petrie, IJSPT 2019, PMC6350656](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6350656); [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)
- Review/proposal for SFMA use in sport — [Aghi et al. IJERPH 2023;20:2032, PMC9915240](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9915240/)

**NASM Overhead Squat Assessment (OHSA) — commercial (NASM certification body), no numeric score**
- NASM's OHSA is a checklist of observed compensations, not a numeric grade: feet (flatten / turn out), knees (move inward), lumbo-pelvic-hip complex (low back arches, excessive forward trunk lean), shoulders (arms fall forward) — [NASM blog, "How to perform an overhead squat assessment"](https://blog.nasm.org/fitness/how-to-perform-an-overhead-squat-assessment-osa); [UKSCA, "Addressing movement patterns by using the overhead squat"](https://cdn.uksca.org.uk/assets/pdfs/UkscaIqPdfs/addressing-movement-patterns-by-using-the-overhead-squat-636825587109500180.pdf)
- Lack of a standard score makes reliability testing harder — [UKSCA](https://cdn.uksca.org.uk/assets/pdfs/UkscaIqPdfs/addressing-movement-patterns-by-using-the-overhead-squat-636825587109500180.pdf)

**Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) — academic (Padua, UNC), free to use**
- 17 items scored from video of a drop jump–rebound landing; items 1–15 are binary (0/1), items 16–17 scored 0–2; because some items are mutually exclusive, the practical maximum is 17 errors; higher = poorer landing mechanics — [University of Waikato thesis/review](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/52a20ee0-a792-4de8-9cde-8fd7e92fb369); [Science for Sport (secondary)](https://www.scienceforsport.com/landing-error-scoring-system-less/)
- Cut-off: in 829 elite youth soccer players (348 boys, 481 girls; age 13.9 ± 1.8 y), ROC analysis gave an optimal cut-point of LESS ≥5 (sensitivity 86%, specificity 64%) for ACL injury; uninjured mean 4.43 ± 1.71 vs injured 6.24 ± 1.75; only 7 ACL injuries occurred — [Padua DA et al. J Athl Train 2015;50(6):589–595, PMC4527442](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4527442)
- LESS-Real Time (LESS-RT): a 10-item abbreviated version scored live; interrater ICC(2,1) 0.72–0.81 in 43 volunteers — [UNF scholars record, "Reliability of the LESS-Real Time"](https://scholars.unf.edu/en/publications/reliability-of-the-landing-error-scoring-system-real-time-a-clini/)

**Y-Balance Test Lower Quarter (YBT-LQ) — commercial device (Plisky / Move2Perform), based on SEBT; brief only**
- Directions: anterior, posteromedial, posterolateral; composite score and anterior reach asymmetry (≥4 cm) are the usual metrics; one study (Butler et al., football) found composite <89.6% → OR 3.5 (95% CI 2.4–5.3) — [Plisky P et al. IJSPT 2021;16(5):1190–1209, doi:10.26603/001c.27634, PMC8486397](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8486397)

### Inferences
- FMS data model for an app: 7 tests × {left, right} where applicable → per-test score = min(L, R); clearing test boolean overrides to 0; total = sum (0–21); store raw sides too (asymmetry = another module). The "heels-raised" deep squat variant and the hand-position variants of the push-up should be explicit fields because they determine 2 vs 3.
- SFMA should be modeled as categorical (FN/FP/DN/DP) per top-tier movement with breakout flow, not a numeric sum.
- OHSA fits a checklist (boolean per compensation per view: anterior/lateral/posterior) with a lookup table of "likely overactive/underactive" muscles — but that lookup is NASM theory, not validated evidence (see Q4).
- LESS: 17 binary/ordinal items summed; display ≥5 as "elevated risk (one youth soccer cohort)" with a caveat about low event counts.

### Gaps
- Exact wording of all 17 LESS items and the original Padua 2009 (Am J Sports Med 37:1996–2002) validation numbers were not retrieved; item list should be pulled from Padua 2009 before implementation.
- The 10 SFMA top-tier movement names and breakout criteria were not retrieved from a primary source here (commonly listed: active cervical flexion, extension, rotation; upper extremity pattern 1 & 2; multi-segmental flexion, extension, rotation; single-leg stance; overhead deep squat — UNVERIFIED in this session; confirm from Stanek 2019 PMC6350656).
- No standard NASM OHSA protocol paper (e.g., Post EG et al., J Sport Rehabil 2017, on observational medial knee displacement during the OHS) was retrieved.

## 3. Evidence on reliability, validity and injury prediction

### Takeaway
FMS composite scoring is reliable (pooled ICCs ~0.84–0.87) but its injury-predictive value is weak and context-dependent: early ≤14 studies suggested ~2–4x risk, but meta-analyses show low sensitivity (~0.24), AUC ~0.58 and heterogeneity; effects appear mainly in senior/male athletes and collision sports (rugby). SFMA reliability depends heavily on rater experience. LESS is reliable overall; ACL predictive value is unclear (one cohort with few injuries). YBT-LQ is reliable but generic cut-points don't predict injury well.

### Cited Findings
**FMS reliability**
- Pooled interrater ICC 0.843 and intrarater ICC 0.869 (moderate evidence of good reliability) are reported in the literature — reported in a search summary as from a 2016 Physical Therapy in Sport meta-analysis; these same figures are commonly attributed to Bonazza et al. 2017 (AJSM) — [Bonazza NM et al. "Reliability, validity, and injury predictive value of the FMS: a systematic review and meta-analysis." Am J Sports Med 2017;45(3):725–732, doi:10.1177/0363546516641937 (Penn State record)](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/reliability-validity-and-injury-predictive-value-of-the-functiona/) — NOTE: attribution conflict, verify in full text.
- Moran et al. systematically reviewed FMS rater reliability (12 studies, QAREL checklist) — [Moran RW et al. "How reliable are FMS scores? A systematic review of rater reliability." BJSM 2016;50(9):527](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/9/527) (conclusions not retrieved).

**FMS validity / injury prediction**
- Bonazza 2017: individuals with composite ≤14 were more likely to be injured; but "significant concerns remain regarding the validity of the FMS" — [Bonazza et al. 2017, AJSM (Penn State record)](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/reliability-validity-and-injury-predictive-value-of-the-functiona/)
- Dorrel et al. 2015: 7 studies in review, 6 in meta-analysis (1,729 cases); pooled sensitivity 0.24 (95% CI 0.15–0.36), specificity 0.85 (0.77–0.91), LR+ 1.65 (1.3–2.0), LR− 0.87 (0.82–0.92), AUC 0.58 (0.42–0.77); cut scores varied across studies; conclusion: "findings do not support the predictive validity of the FMS" — [Dorrel BS, Long T, Shaffer S, Myer GD. Sports Health 2015;7(6):532–537, doi:10.1177/1941738115607445, PMC4622382](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4622382)
- Moore et al. 2019 (29 studies for composite score; PROSPERO CRD42018092916): pooled OR for low composite score — junior athletes 1.03 (0.67–1.59, NS); senior 1.80 (1.17–2.78); males 1.79 (1.08–2.96); females 1.92 (0.43–8.56, NS); rugby 5.92 (1.67–20.92); American football 4.41 (0.94–20.61, NS); ice hockey 3.70 (0.89–15.42, NS). Specificity > sensitivity for composite score. Conclusion: age, sex and sport explain some inconsistency; effect sizes generally small — [Moore E, Chalmers S, Milanese S, Fuller JT. Sports Med 2019;49(9):1449–1463, doi:10.1007/s40279-019-01126-5](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/factors-influencing-the-relationship-between-the-functional-movem/)
- Trinidad-Fernández et al. 2019: overall OR 1.86 (95% CI 1.32–2.61) for ≤14 but substantial heterogeneity (I² = 70%); concluded it is unclear whether ≤14/21 is associated with increased injury risk — [BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med 2019, doi:10.1136/bmjsem-2018-000501](https://dx.doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2018-000501)
- Moran et al. 2017 BJSM meta-analysis "Do FMS composite scores predict subsequent injury?" exists (BJSM 51(23):1661) — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/51/23/1661) — results not retrieved (403).

**SFMA reliability**
- 10 top-tier movements, categorical scoring: intra-rater kappa 0.21–1.00 (absolute agreement 0.64–1.00); inter-rater kappa 0.11–0.89 (agreement 0.52–0.96); raters with most SFMA certification/experience were most reliable — [Stanek JM, Smith J, Petrie J. IJSPT 2019, PMC6350656](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6350656)
- Student rater intra-rater kappa 0.49 (moderate); senior physio vs student inter-rater kappa 0.60 before / 0.62 after training — [Aghi et al. IJERPH 2023, PMC9915240](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9915240/)
- Novice PT students after 4-h training were studied (Harper & Aron, IJSPT 2023) — [IJSPT PDF](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/82173.pdf) (values not retrieved).

**LESS**
- Overall LESS score shows good-to-excellent intrarater, interrater and intersession reliability; concurrent validity of individual items vs 3D motion capture is item-dependent; value for predicting ACL injury is unclear — [Waikato research commons (systematic review)](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/52a20ee0-a792-4de8-9cde-8fd7e92fb369)
- Only prospective ACL cut-point evidence found: Padua 2015 (≥5, Se 86%, Sp 64%, n = 7 injuries) — [PMC4527442](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4527442)

**YBT-LQ (brief)**
- Intra-rater ICC 0.85–0.91 (lower in adolescents 0.57–0.82), inter-rater 0.81–1.00, test-retest 0.63–0.93; only 1 of 13 studies linked composite score to injury, 3 linked anterior asymmetry; "using general cut points, the test may not predict injury"; interpret by age, sex and sport — [Plisky et al. IJSPT 2021, PMC8486397](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8486397)

**NASM OHSA**
- No study specifically testing reliability of the NASM OHSA as a whole was found in this session — [search result summary; see NASM blog](https://blog.nasm.org/fitness/how-to-perform-an-overhead-squat-assessment-osa)

**MSI (Sahrmann)**
- Inter-tester reliability of MSI low back pain classification with a standardized exam was "substantial" (2 trained therapists, 30 patients); novice raters had moderate-to-good item reliability — [WUSTL profile: inter-tester reliability study](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-inter-tester-reliability-of-physical-therapists-classifying-l/); [WUSTL profile: novice raters](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/reliability-of-novice-raters-in-using-the-movement-system-impairm/)
- A systematic review of chronic LBP classification systems found MSI interrater reliability substantial but risk of bias high; insufficient evidence to conclude — [James Cook University record](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/33509/)

### Inferences
- In the app, FMS total should NOT be labeled "injury risk" without caveats; a defensible label is "movement quality screen; composite ≤14 has been associated with modestly higher injury odds in some adult/senior athlete populations; poor sensitivity (misses ~3/4 of future injuries)."
- Commercial-vs-independent: FMS/SFMA and YBT originate from developers with commercial interests (Cook, Plisky are co-authors of the founding/review papers). Independent meta-analyses (Dorrel, Moore, Trinidad-Fernández, Moran) are the more skeptical ones — flag this in source attribution.
- Reliability depends on training/certification (SFMA, MSI) → app could store rater certification level and support video-based re-scoring.

### Gaps
- Moran 2017 BJSM pooled values and GRADE rating not retrieved (paywall/403).
- Bonazza 2017 pooled injury OR not retrieved; reliability ICC attribution conflict noted above.
- No 2023–2026 umbrella review on FMS injury prediction found in this session; current (2026) consensus inferred from 2015–2019 meta-analyses.
- LESS ACL prediction meta-analytic pooled estimate not found.

## 4. Common movement faults/compensations per pattern, proposed causes and corrections

### Takeaway
Fault lists are well codified (FMS criteria, NASM OHSA checklist, LESS items), but the causal "fault → muscle imbalance → corrective exercise" mappings come mostly from commercial/educational sources (NASM, FMS) and are theory-driven, not validated by strong trials.

### Cited Findings
- Squat (FMS criteria imply faults): heels must be raised to complete (attributed to limited ankle dorsiflexion or thoracic extension); lumbar flexion; torso not parallel to tibia; knees not over feet; dowel drifting forward — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)
- Overhead squat (NASM): feet flatten/turn out, knees move inward (valgus), low back arches, excessive forward trunk lean, arms fall forward — [NASM blog](https://blog.nasm.org/fitness/how-to-perform-an-overhead-squat-assessment-osa)
- Knee valgus proposed causes (NASM): decreased hip abductor and hip external rotator strength, increased hip adductor activity, restricted ankle dorsiflexion; NASM frames the injury link as a hypothesis — [NASM blog](https://blog.nasm.org/fitness/how-to-perform-an-overhead-squat-assessment-osa)
- Lunge/split stance (FMS): loss of dowel contact or verticality, torso movement, feet/dowel leaving sagittal plane, knee not reaching board, loss of balance — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)
- Single-leg step (FMS hurdle step): hip-knee-ankle misalignment, lumbar movement, dowel tilting, hurdle contact, loss of balance — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4060319/)
- Push / anti-extension (FMS TSPU): lumbar "lag" (body not rising as one unit) — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4127517)
- Rotation / anti-rotation (FMS rotary stability): inability to perform unilateral or diagonal pattern while keeping weight centered — [Cook et al. 2014 Part 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4127517)
- Hinge: hinge is defined by hip-dominant motion with a neutral spine, so lumbar flexion substituting for hip flexion is the defining fault — [Strength for Longevity](https://strengthforlongevity.substack.com/p/exercise-series-the-hinge-and-squat) (practitioner source)
- Landing (LESS): errors include knee valgus, limited knee/hip flexion ("stiff" landing), trunk position, stance width, asymmetric foot contact; higher totals = poorer mechanics; the key ACL-risk items were reported as valid — [Waikato](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/52a20ee0-a792-4de8-9cde-8fd7e92fb369); [Padua 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4527442)
- Corrective strategy: NASM suggests how to minimize the effect of each compensation (inhibit/lengthen overactive, activate/strengthen underactive, integrate) — [UKSCA](https://cdn.uksca.org.uk/assets/pdfs/UkscaIqPdfs/addressing-movement-patterns-by-using-the-overhead-squat-636825587109500180.pdf); [Brookbush Institute (commercial education)](https://brookbushinstitute.com/articles/sign-clusters-compensation-patterns-overhead-squat-assessment)
- Limitation: screens not performed under load or at high velocity lack sport specificity — [UKSCA](https://cdn.uksca.org.uk/assets/pdfs/UkscaIqPdfs/addressing-movement-patterns-by-using-the-overhead-squat-636825587109500180.pdf)
- Sahrmann's MSI approach classifies patients by the direction of movement/alignment that provokes symptoms (e.g., lumbar extension, rotation) and treats by correcting that direction — [Van Dillen et al., stage 1 validation](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/movement-system-impairment-based-categories-for-low-back-pain-sta/)

### Inferences
- An app "fault library" can be structured as Pattern → Fault (observable, view: front/side/back) → Proposed contributors (mobility / strength / control) → Suggested regressions/corrective drills, with an evidence-level tag (e.g., "expert/commercial model" vs "RCT-supported").
- Fault definitions are more reliable to implement than the causal mappings; keep the latter editable by clinicians.

### Gaps
- No systematic review on effectiveness of corrective-exercise programs (e.g., FMS-based corrective exercise improving scores or reducing injury) was retrieved this session; needs a targeted search.
- Full NASM "overactive/underactive muscle" table not retrieved from a primary NASM text (Essentials of Corrective Exercise Training); avoid hard-coding without verification.
- No sourced fault list for carry, pull, or gait patterns was retrieved.

## 5. APTA "movement system" as PT's identity and diagnosis/classification systems

### Takeaway
APTA (2013) adopted the vision "Transforming society by optimizing movement to improve the human experience," with "identity" as the first guiding principle: PT defines and promotes the movement system. APTA intends to shift from medical diagnoses to movement-system diagnoses, adopted criteria for developing them, but explicitly does not endorse any single classification system. Sahrmann's MSI is the best-known movement-system diagnostic model; others exist (not fully covered here).

### Cited Findings
- APTA vision (2013): "Transforming society by optimizing movement to improve the human experience" — [Saladin L, Voight M. "Introduction to the movement system as the foundation for physical therapist practice education and research." IJSPT 2017;12(6):858 (PDF, Belmont)](https://news.belmont.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/IJSPT-Saladin.pdf)
- Identity guiding principle: "The physical therapy profession will define and promote the movement system as the foundation for optimizing movement to improve the health of society"; PTs evaluate and manage the movement system across the lifespan, "diagnose impairments, activity limitations, and participation restrictions," and "the movement system is the core of physical therapist practice, education, and research" — [Saladin & Voight 2017](https://news.belmont.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/IJSPT-Saladin.pdf)
- Definition: the movement system is the collection of systems (cardiovascular, pulmonary, endocrine, integumentary, nervous, musculoskeletal) that interact to move the body or its component parts — [Saladin & Voight 2017](https://news.belmont.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/IJSPT-Saladin.pdf); [APTA movement system white paper (PDF)](https://www.APTA.org/contentassets/fadbcf0476484eba9b790c9567435817/movement-system-white-paper.pdf)
- Goals of adopting the movement system: (1) elevate public perception (knowledge, not just techniques), (2) reduce unwarranted practice variation, (3) promote new diagnostic labels meaningful to PT and guiding treatment, (4) unify entry-level education around a common core — [Saladin & Voight 2017](https://news.belmont.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/IJSPT-Saladin.pdf)
- APTA intends to move from medical diagnoses toward movement-system diagnoses, "has adopted criteria for the development of movement system diagnoses," but has "absolutely no intention of adopting, endorsing or supporting any single therapeutic approach or diagnostic classification system" — [Saladin & Voight 2017](https://news.belmont.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/IJSPT-Saladin.pdf)
- Critique: Sahrmann (2022, IJSPT) argues the "doctor of the movement system"/"diagnostician" identity has limited recognition because expected abilities were never defined; identity must be earned by choice rather than held by default as "therapists providing treatment" — [Sahrmann, IJSPT, "Doctors of the movement system: identity by choice or therapists providing treatment: identity by default"](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/30175-doctors-of-the-movement-system-identity-by-choice-or-therapists-providing-treatment-identity-by-default)
- Orthopaedic section (AOPT) materials restate PTs' role in diagnosing within the movement system — [AOPT, "Moving Forward with the Movement System" (slides)](https://www.orthopt.org/uploads/content_files/files/Moving%20Forward%20with%20the%20Movement%20System.pdf)
- MSI (Sahrmann) low back categories and reliability — see Q1/Q3: [stage 1 validation](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/movement-system-impairment-based-categories-for-low-back-pain-sta/); [inter-tester reliability](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-inter-tester-reliability-of-physical-therapists-classifying-l/)
- MSI categories have also been applied to mechanical neck pain (reliability of diagnostic tests studied) — [Arch Bone Jt Surg, article 25228](https://abjs.mums.ac.ir/article_25228.html)
- Other LBP classification systems (e.g., O'Sullivan Classification System, OCS) show fair-to-almost-perfect interrater reliability, but overall evidence insufficient due to high risk of bias — [JCU systematic review](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/33509/)

### Inferences
- For the app, a "movement system diagnosis" field should support multiple classification schemas (MSI, TBC, OCS, MDT, ICF-based) rather than hard-coding one, consistent with APTA's non-endorsement stance.
- The ICF triad (impairments, activity limitations, participation restrictions) in the APTA principle is a natural top-level data structure for assessment outputs.
